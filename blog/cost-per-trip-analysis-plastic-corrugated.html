Cost-Per-Trip Analysis: Plastic Corrugated Boxes vs. Single-Use Cardboard

Plasticcorrugatedbox Editorial • September 2026

Why "Cost Per Box" Is the Wrong Metric

When procurement teams evaluate packaging options, the default comparison is unit price: how much does one box cost? For single-use packaging, this is a reasonable starting point. For reusable plastic corrugated boxes — where the same unit will absorb many shipping cycles before retirement — unit price is almost meaningless as a decision metric. The number that actually matters is cost per trip: total ownership cost divided by the number of cycles a container reliably delivers.

This analysis walks through how to calculate cost per trip for plastic corrugated boxes, what factors drive that number up or down, and how to use it to make a defensible procurement case internally.

The Cost-Per-Trip Formula

At its simplest, cost per trip for a reusable plastic corrugated box is:

CPT = (Acquisition Cost + Lifetime Maintenance) ÷ Total Cycles Delivered

Each variable requires real numbers, not estimates:

  • Acquisition cost: Full landed cost per unit, including freight, tariffs, and any setup or tooling fees for custom sizes
  • Lifetime maintenance: Cleaning cost per cycle — labor, water, chemicals — multiplied by total cycles
  • Total cycles: The rated cycle life from the manufacturer, adjusted for your specific handling conditions and attrition rate

For a standard twin-wall PP corrugated box with a $12 landed cost, 250 rated cycles, and $0.08/cycle cleaning cost:

CPT = ($12 + 250 × $0.08) ÷ 250 = $0.128 per trip

Compare that to a single-use cardboard alternative at $1.20 per box, and you're looking at a 90% reduction in per-trip packaging cost — before accounting for cardboard disposal fees.

What Drives Cycle Life (and What Cuts It Short)

The cycle life of a plastic corrugated box isn't fixed — it depends on how the boxes are handled, stored, and maintained. The most common reasons boxes fall short of their rated cycle life:

  • Improper stacking: Overloading beyond rated stack weight causes delamination of twin-wall flutes
  • UV exposure: Outdoor storage degrades unstabilized PP over time, making boxes brittle
  • Harsh cleaning chemistry: Strong oxidizers or caustic washes above the rated temperature maximum accelerate surface degradation
  • Mechanical damage: Forklift punctures and corner impacts from pallet drops are typically the biggest attrition driver in high-throughput warehouses

Building a realistic attrition factor — typically 5–15% per year depending on operational intensity — into your cost model gives a more honest CPT figure than assuming every box reaches its rated maximum cycles.

Comparing Cardboard vs. Plastic Corrugated: A Side-by-Side Model

For a distribution operation moving 500 boxes per day, 250 operating days per year:

  • Cardboard: 125,000 boxes/year × $1.20 = $150,000/year. Disposal costs: ~$8,000/year. 3-year total: $474,000
  • Plastic corrugated (reusable): Fleet of 5,000 units × $12 = $60,000. Cleaning at $0.08 × 125,000 cycles/year = $10,000/year. Annual attrition (8%): $4,800/year. 3-year total: $60,000 + $44,400 = $104,400

Factoring In Tariff Position

For US buyers, acquisition cost is significantly affected by country of origin. PP corrugated boxes manufactured in China carry Section 301 tariffs that add 25% or more to landed cost. Elipacko produces from Thailand and Vietnam — both at 0% additional duty under current tariff schedules — which keeps landed acquisition cost competitive without requiring buyers to absorb tariff-driven price increases.

When building your CPT model, use your actual landed cost including freight, duty, and brokerage fees, not the FOB price. A lower FOB from a tariff-affected country often inverts the advantage once duty is applied at the port of entry.

Custom Sizing and Its Effect on Total Cost

Standard box sizes offer lower tooling costs and faster availability, but they're not always optimal for your operation. A box that is 15% oversized for your typical product means wasted cube, inefficient pallet utilization, and higher per-trip shipping cost that may offset the packaging savings.

Building the Internal Business Case

For procurement managers presenting a switch from single-use to reusable plastic corrugated to finance or operations leadership, the CPT model is the most compelling framing. Pair it with current annual cardboard spend pulled from accounts payable, disposal and recycling costs often buried in facilities budgets, any ESG or sustainability reporting requirements the packaging change would support, and handling and damage data from your current supplier.

A well-built CPT analysis converts a packaging decision from a cost conversation into an investment conversation — and the numbers consistently support reusable PP corrugated for any operation running more than 200 trips per year per container.
